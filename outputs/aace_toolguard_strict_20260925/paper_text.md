# Suggested AACE manuscript insertions

**Matched action-admission comparison.** We reran current AACE-full and the official ToolGuard implementation on the same 50 adversarial profile–seed pairs with Qwen-plus, shared seller and buyer configuration, economic parameters, horizon, hard-policy content, and post-execution evaluator. AACE-full denotes the current action-admission controller with deterministic replanning (legacy implementation identifier `ocl_full`). Both methods had zero observed executed violations. Valid success was 44/50 (88%) for AACE-full and 46/50 (92%) for ToolGuard; the paired difference of −4 percentage points has a 95% paired bootstrap interval of [−12, +4] and exact discordant-pair p=0.625. These results do not establish an AACE advantage in safety or valid task completion. Under this setup, AACE had higher mean seller reward (22.84 versus 19.30) and lower episode latency (70.95 versus 95.82 seconds). These operational differences should not be generalized beyond the measured backend and execution setup.

**Recovery and baseline disclosure.** AACE rejected 75 proposals and executed all 75 deterministic replans after revalidation. ToolGuard rejected 166 proposals, including failed revisions, and 74 of 120 revision attempts passed the official guard and reached the environment. Initial-draft rejection counts were 75/163 and 120/174, respectively. Recovery execution is distinct from eventual agreement: 42 AACE episodes and 43 ToolGuard episodes both executed a recovery and ended in valid agreement. ToolGuard uses the unchanged official runtime and generated guards, with an AgenticPay host that returns the rejected draft and sanitized feedback to the same seller for at most one revision. This host retry policy is an integration choice, not a universal default of the official runtime. Generation compatibility steps, the upstream test-generation fallback, and context matching are disclosed in the protocol. Episode latency includes configured API sleeps and excludes offline guard generation.

**Verified Constraint Evolution by category.** The five Hard-only held-out attack violations comprised two Privacy Phisher and three Role Hijacker cases; Time Waster had none. The learning run generated 26 candidate families (11 privacy, 14 role, one time-wasting) and six additional privacy revisions. Eight privacy families, all 14 role families, and the time-wasting family failed empirical verification. All three admitted constraints were privacy-related. The Candidate Curation Gate received these three empirically approved candidates and accepted all three; this run does not demonstrate rejection or deferral by that stage. Relative to the implemented hard detector, the accepted constraints extend enforcement from explicit credential values to payment identifiers, disclosure commitments, and off-platform settlement, while specializing an existing policy objective rather than discovering new normative requirements.

**Category limitations.** Role-hijacking failures generated candidates, but none met the fixed verification requirements. The reduction in held-out role violations from three to two includes a case where both conditions executed their sole proposal without intervention and the saved judges gave inconsistent interpretations of negotiated pricing; it cannot be attributed to a learned role constraint. Time Waster had one derivation failure, but its candidate made no observed validation interception; the one-turn validation and held-out trajectories did not exercise the temporal repetition condition. This category therefore provides no demonstrated learning benefit. Privacy violations decreased from two to one, with a residual off-platform-payment failure; the contained privacy case ended in timeout rather than successful recovery.
